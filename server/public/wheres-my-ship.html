How it works (and one honest caveat)

Ships report their position constantly over a radio system called AIS, and we listen two ways. Near coastlines and islands, shore receivers hear them around the clock — and since cruise itineraries mostly hug the coast, that covers the Caribbean, the Mediterranean, Alaska, Northern Europe, Asia, South America, you name it. Out in open water, shore receivers go quiet — think the run from Miami to Nassau, a transatlantic crossing, or a long sea day. When a ship has gone quiet, we ask a satellite where she is the moment you select her; satellite reports arrive minutes to a few hours apart, so between reports the map shows her estimated position, projected along her route at her last speed, and the marker says so. The moment a new report lands, live or satellite, the marker snaps to it. She didn't go anywhere she shouldn't — she's just between reports.

Why is my ship's position old, or marked as estimated?

Ships report over a radio system called AIS. Shore receivers hear them within a couple hundred miles of land, and satellites hear them everywhere, but satellite positions arrive minutes to a few hours apart and we pull one the moment you select a ship whose shore report has gone stale. Between reports the map shows her estimated position, projected along her route at her last speed, and says so on the marker. The moment a new report lands, the marker snaps to it.

Can I use the tracker on embarkation day?

Yes, and it is the best use of it. If your ship was due at the pier at 6 a.m. and she is still inbound at 7, boarding will run late, and you can have a second coffee instead of standing in line. Check the arrival-day weather card next to the map before you leave the hotel. The cruise line's own alerts are the final word on boarding times; this page is your early warning.
